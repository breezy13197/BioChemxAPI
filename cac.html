Module 1

The gateway to the mitochondria

Before the citric acid cycle can run, carbon has to get into it. This module follows pyruvate from the end of glycolysis to the moment it becomes acetyl-CoA, and takes apart the enzyme complex that makes that conversion happen.

By the end of this module you should be able to

  • Place each step of glucose oxidation in the correct compartment
  • Assign each of the three PDH enzymes to its coenzyme, and each coenzyme to its vitamin
  • Track carbon, the acetyl group, and electrons through the PDH reaction
  • Predict whether the gateway is open or closed from a cell's energy state
Module 1 · Section 1

Where does pyruvate go?

START HERE Before you read on

Glycolysis has just finished. Oxygen is available and the cell needs ATP. Decide, before scrolling, where pyruvate has to travel next and why it cannot simply be used where it already is. Hold that answer — the activity below will test it.

Glucose oxidation is split across two compartments, and the split is not arbitrary. Glycolysis runs in the cytosol. Every enzyme of the citric acid cycle, and the entire pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, sits in the mitochondrial matrix. Pyruvate therefore has to be carried across both mitochondrial membranes before anything else can happen to it.

Sort the pathway into its compartments

Drag each step into the compartment where it actually takes place. One of them is deliberately awkward — think about where the enzyme physically sits, not where its substrate came from.

WHY IT MATTERS One-way traffic

The conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is irreversible. Carbon that crosses this line can be oxidized to CO2 or built into lipid, but it cannot be turned back into pyruvate and therefore cannot be used to make net glucose. That single fact will come back when you study gluconeogenesis.

Module 1 · Section 2

Build the PDH complex

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is three enzymes working in sequence, using five coenzymes between them. Rather than memorizing the list, it helps to see what each enzyme physically needs in order to do its part of the job.

EnzymeJobCoenzyme
E1 · Pyruvate dehydrogenase Removes CO2, holds the two-carbon fragment Thiamine pyrophosphate
E2 · Dihydrolipoyl transacetylase Swings the acetyl group across and hands it to CoA Lipoic acid (plus CoA as the acceptor)
E3 · Dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase Restores oxidized lipoamide so the complex can run again FAD, then NAD+

The cofactor repair shop

A batch of PDH complexes has been assembled with the coenzymes in the wrong places. Give each enzyme the coenzyme it actually needs, then do the same for the vitamin each coenzyme comes from.

WHY IT MATTERS Four vitamins in one complex

B1, B2, B3 and B5 are all represented here, and B1 is required twice over in this pathway — once at PDH and again at the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex. That double dependence is why poor thiamine status has such a broad effect on oxidative metabolism, raising lactate while ATP production falls.

Follow the carbons and the electrons

Three things move through the PDH reaction at once, and it is worth watching all three rather than only counting carbons. Step through the reaction below and watch the carbon skeleton shrink, the acetyl group form, and the electrons collect on NAD+.

A COMMON MIX-UP Keep the PDH CO2 separate

One CO2 leaves at PDH. Two more leave later, inside the cycle, at isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase. Students who merge these end up crediting the cycle with three CO2 per acetyl-CoA instead of two. Keep the two ledgers apart now and the bookkeeping in Module 2 becomes much easier.

HIGH YIELD One enzyme, two pathways

Succinate dehydrogenase is the only enzyme that belongs to both the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain, where it serves as Complex II. Its FAD stays bound to the enzyme and passes electrons straight to coenzyme Q, skipping Complex I. That shortcut is a large part of why FADH2 supports less ATP synthesis than NADH does.

KEEP THESE APART Two separate ledgers

Pyruvate processing and the cycle are usually taught together, so their products are easy to merge. Per glucose, the PDH complex contributes 2 NADH and 2 CO2. The cycle itself contributes 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 ATP or GTP and 4 CO2. Six CO2 total — which is exactly the six carbons of the original glucose.

Module 2 · Section 5

The malate dehydrogenase paradox

START HERE A reaction that should not run

Malate → oxaloacetate has a positive standard free energy change. On paper, the reverse direction is favored. And yet the cycle turns continuously. Before you read on, write down what you think is happening.

Standard free energy is measured with substrates and products at equal concentration. Cells are not like that. What actually determines direction is the concentration of the product relative to the substrate — and the cell can control that by consuming the product as fast as it is made.

Turn citrate synthase on and off

Watch what happens to oxaloacetate, and to net flux through malate dehydrogenase, when the next enzyme in the cycle stops removing the product.

Module 3

Metabolic traffic control

The cycle does not run at a fixed speed. Four enzymes act as throttles, and they all respond to the same underlying question: does this cell need more ATP right now? This module is built around prediction rather than recall — you will set a cell's state, commit to an answer, and then run it.

Module 3 · Section 1

The four control points

Regulation happens where reactions are far from equilibrium. Steps that sit near equilibrium can be sped up or slowed down without much changing net flux, so the cell does not bother controlling them. That single principle predicts which four enzymes are regulated here.

Toggle the signal, watch the enzymes respond

Turn each signal on and see which of the four control points is activated or inhibited. Look for the pattern rather than memorizing the grid.

THINK IT THROUGH Why succinyl-CoA inhibits citrate synthase

Succinyl-CoA sits four steps downstream of citrate. If it is accumulating, carbon is entering the cycle faster than the later steps can process it. Slowing the entry reaction is the sensible response — this is feedback by a downstream intermediate, not by a final product.

Module 3 · Section 3

The PDH phosphorylation switch

START HERE Commit before you click

A kinase adds a phosphate. A phosphatase removes one. Before you touch the switch below, decide which of those two leaves the PDH complex active. Most students guess the wrong way round on first meeting, which is exactly why it is worth predicting first and being corrected immediately.

Unlike the other three control points, the PDH complex carries a covalent switch on top of its allosteric regulation. That extra layer exists because the reaction it guards is an irreversible commitment.

WHY IT MATTERS Two layers, one decision

Allosteric regulation adjusts activity from moment to moment. Covalent modification sets a longer-lasting position. A cell that has been energy-replete for some time keeps PDH phosphorylated and off; one that starts working dephosphorylates it and reopens the gate. Same enzyme, two timescales of control.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase phosphorylates the PDH complex. What is the result?

Which enzyme restores activity to the phosphorylated PDH complex?

Module 4 · Section 3

Alternative entry points

Pyruvate carboxylase is the main anaplerotic route, but not the only one. Amino acid carbon and odd-chain fatty acid carbon both reach the cycle, each entering at a specific intermediate.

CLINICAL LINK Methylmalonic acid and B12

Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase needs cobalamin to convert methylmalonyl-CoA into succinyl-CoA. When B12 is deficient the substrate backs up and methylmalonic acid accumulates, which is why measuring it helps separate B12 deficiency from folate deficiency — the two otherwise look similar on a blood count.

Module 5

Oxygen, redox balance and metabolic fate

Here is the misconception this module exists to dismantle: the citric acid cycle does not use oxygen, so why does it stop when oxygen disappears? Answering that properly requires seeing the cycle as one part of a larger system rather than as a self-contained loop.

By the end of this module you should be able to

  • Trace what happens to NADH and NAD+ when the chain stalls
  • Describe the fate of pyruvate with and without oxygen, and why
  • Attribute the 30 to 32 ATP figure to the right process

Module 5 · Section 2

Follow pyruvate

Pyruvate sits at a branch point, and which road it takes is decided almost entirely by whether NAD+ can be regenerated. Open each door below and follow the consequences.

WORTH NAMING Lactate makes no ATP

Reducing pyruvate to lactate produces no ATP at all. Its entire purpose is to reoxidize NADH so that glycolysis — which does make ATP, two per glucose — can keep running. Ethanol fermentation serves the same purpose in yeast and some bacteria, but is not available to human tissue.

Module 5 · Section 3

ATP efficiency challenge

One glucose enters. Watch where the ATP actually accumulates under each condition, and pay attention to which process each token comes from — the headline number hides a distinction worth keeping.

SAY IT PRECISELY From the whole process

Roughly 30 to 32 ATP per glucose comes from glycolysis, pyruvate processing, the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation combined. The cycle itself makes only two ATP or GTP per glucose directly. Its real contribution is the six NADH and two FADH2 it hands to the electron transport chain.
