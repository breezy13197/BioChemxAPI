Module 1

Amino Acid Economy

Where do amino acids come from, and when does the body break them down?

Before tracking a single nitrogen atom, it helps to know the budget: which amino acids you can build, why protein gets degraded at all, how dietary protein is safely digested, and which cofactors show up again and again.

Module 1 · Section 1

Essential or synthesizable?

Humans can build 11 of the 20 proteinogenic amino acids, borrowing carbon skeletons from glucose metabolism. The other 9 have no human biosynthetic pathway and must come from food. Because a protein cannot be finished if even one of its amino acids is missing, the essential ones are what set the ceiling on protein synthesis.

Module 1 · Section 2

When amino acids are degraded

Amino acids are catabolized in three situations: during normal protein turnover, when the diet supplies more protein than synthesis needs, and when carbohydrate is unavailable or cannot be properly used. The first two happen every day. The third is what turns protein, including your muscle, into fuel.

A key reason for the second: there is no storage form for amino acids. Glucose has glycogen and fatty acids have triacylglycerol, but surplus amino acids have nowhere to wait.

Module 1 · Section 3

Proteolysis & zymogens

Protein digestion starts in the stomach, where acid unfolds dietary proteins so proteases can reach their peptide bonds, and it finishes in the small intestine. No single protease can fully digest a protein; pepsin, trypsin, and chymotrypsin each cut at different places. That raises an obvious problem: how does a cell make an enzyme this destructive without digesting itself?

Module 2

Follow the Nitrogen

What happens to the amino group after an amino acid is broken down?

Free ammonia is toxic, especially to the brain, so the body never lets nitrogen wander. It is captured onto a carrier, shipped safely through the blood, released in the liver, and packaged into urea. This module follows one nitrogen atom through each of those jobs, in order: capture → transport → dispose → regulate.

Module 2 · Section 1

Capture the nitrogen: transamination

The first move is almost never to release ammonia. Instead, an aminotransferase swaps groups between two molecules: the amino acid gives its amino group to an α-keto acid, and each becomes the other kind of molecule. PLP is the go-between, briefly holding the amino group as pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP).

Most amino groups end up on the same acceptor, α-ketoglutarate, which makes glutamate the collecting point for nitrogen from nearly every amino acid.

Module 2 · Section 2

Transport the nitrogen

Muscle breaks down amino acids for energy, but it cannot make urea. The nitrogen has to reach the liver without ever circulating as free ammonia. Two carriers do this job: alanine, which also brings the liver a carbon skeleton for glucose (the glucose–alanine, or Cahill, cycle), and glutamine, which carries two nitrogens and is the most abundant amino acid in blood.

Module 2 · Section 3

Dispose of the nitrogen: the urea cycle

Urea carries two nitrogen atoms out of the body, and they arrive by different doors. One comes in as free NH4+ inside the mitochondrion; the other is carried in by aspartate in the cytosol. The cycle spans both compartments, which is why citrulline and ornithine have to cross the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Module 2 · Section 4

Regulate & troubleshoot

The urea cycle has to speed up after a high-protein meal and slow down during a low-protein day. Its main control point is the first enzyme, CPS I, which barely works without its allosteric activator, N-acetylglutamate (NAG). When the cycle fails, the pattern of what piles up before the block is the clue to where the block is.

CLINICAL CONNECTION Where the extra carbamoyl phosphate goes

When OTC is deficient, carbamoyl phosphate cannot enter the cycle. It leaks into the cytosol and feeds pyrimidine synthesis, so orotic acid appears in the urine. That one lab value separates OTC deficiency from CPS I or NAG synthase deficiency, which look alike otherwise. (You will meet orotic acid again in the Nucleotides companion.)

Module 3

Follow the Carbon

Once the nitrogen is gone, what happens to the carbon skeleton, and what can it become?

Twenty amino acids have twenty degradation pathways, but those pathways empty into just seven intermediates. Learn the seven stations and you can predict an amino acid’s fate without memorizing a list. This module builds that skill in three levels:

Level 1 · DestinationWhere does the carbon skeleton enter central metabolism?
Level 2 · ConsequenceCan carbon from that station make glucose, ketone bodies, or both?
Level 3 · ClassificationOnly now: is the amino acid glucogenic, ketogenic, or both?
Module 3 · Section 1

The seven destinations

Pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, acetoacetyl-CoA, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate, and oxaloacetate. Four of these are citric acid cycle intermediates; the other three sit just upstream. Some amino acids break into more than one piece and arrive at two or three stations.

The metabolic subway map

Module 3 · Section 2

Glucose or ketone bodies?

Here is the one rule that decides everything in this module: carbon that reaches oxaloacetate can become glucose; carbon that enters as acetyl-CoA cannot. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible, and every two carbons that enter the citric acid cycle as acetyl-CoA are matched by two carbons leaving as CO2. No net oxaloacetate, no net glucose.

Module 3 · Section 3

Classify the amino acids

Classification is now just bookkeeping. If any product is pyruvate or a citric acid cycle intermediate, the amino acid is glucogenic. If any product is acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA, it is ketogenic. If it has one of each, it is both.

A NOTE ON THREONINE

On the Lehninger map in the lecture, threonine appears at pyruvate and succinyl-CoA. It is still classified as both glucogenic and ketogenic because part of its carbon can also be cleaved to acetyl-CoA (shown in the lecture’s summary map). This companion follows the lecture’s classification.

Module 4

Build an Amino Acid

How does the body build the nonessential amino acids?

Biosynthesis runs on the same intermediates you just used for breakdown. Instead of tearing a carbon skeleton down to α-ketoglutarate or oxaloacetate, the cell starts there and builds up, adding nitrogen and, sometimes, sulfur. The goal of this module is not to memorize enzymes but to read a reaction and predict its enzyme, its class, and why it needs what it needs.

Reaction lab

Enzyme names follow rules. A synthetase spends ATP to join two things (a ligase). An -ase that uses water to split a bond is a hydrolase. A dehydrogenase moves electrons (an oxidoreductase). A synthase makes something without spending ATP, and that can be a lyase or a transferase. Apply the rules to six reactions from the lecture.

Module 4 · Section 3

Methionine, SAM & cysteine

Methionine is essential, yet the body recycles it constantly. Activated as SAM, it donates methyl groups to build molecules like epinephrine and melatonin. What remains is homocysteine, which sits at a fork: regenerate methionine (needs B12 and folate), or commit to making cysteine (CBS, switched on by high SAM).

Module 5

Build Something Else

How do amino acids become neurotransmitters, hormones, and protective molecules?

Amino acids are not only protein building blocks and fuel. A few short, cofactor-driven pathways turn them into catecholamines, serotonin, melatonin, histamine, and glutathione. The same handful of reaction types keeps repeating: hydroxylation (BH4), decarboxylation (PLP), and methylation (SAM). Spot the reaction type and you can predict the cofactor, and predict what breaks when it is missing.

Module 5 · Section 1

Module 5 · Section 2

Serotonin & melatonin

You do not need to learn the tryptophan pathway from scratch. It uses the same three reaction types as the catecholamine pathway: hydroxylate the ring (BH4), remove CO2 (PLP, and even the same enzyme), then later add a methyl group (SAM). Serotonin acts at 5-HT receptors; melatonin, made from serotonin, helps regulate the sleep–wake cycle.

Module 5 · Section 3

Histamine & glutathione

Histamine is one decarboxylation away from histidine, made by its own dedicated enzyme, histidine decarboxylase. It acts on histamine receptors to drive allergic and inflammatory responses, which is where antihistamines act.

Glutathione is a tripeptide that protects cells against reactive oxygen species. Its power comes from one atom: the sulfur of cysteine.
