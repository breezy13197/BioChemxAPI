Module 1

Follow the electrons

Central question: where do the electrons go, and what happens because they move?

Oxidative phosphorylation runs in two phases. This module is phase one: electrons leave NADH and FADH₂, travel through four protein complexes, and end up in water — and the energy released along the way is spent pushing protons out of the matrix.

You will not be handed the finished diagram. You will build it, then work out the proton arithmetic yourself.

By the end of this module you should be able to

  • Explain why electrons move from NADH toward oxygen using reduction potential
  • Place complexes I–IV, coenzyme Q, and cytochrome c in the correct order
  • Distinguish the NADH entry route from the FADH₂ entry route
  • Derive 10 H⁺ per NADH and 6 H⁺ per FADH₂ from the per-complex stoichiometry
Module 1 · Section 1

Why electrons move

Nothing pushes electrons through the chain. They fall. Each carrier holds electrons a little more tightly than the one before it, and that difference — reduction potential — is what makes every transfer spontaneous.

The overall reaction is the same one that makes fire hot:

The chain exists to release this energy gradually, in steps small enough to do useful work.

The reason the mitochondrion does not simply run this reaction directly is that the energy would leave as heat. Instead, the drop is divided across a series of carriers, and three of those steps are coupled to pumping protons out of the matrix.

Building the chain

There are two doors into the electron transport chain, and which door the electrons use determines almost everything that follows. Rather than reading the finished pathway, build it.

MANIPULATE · BUILD THE ELECTRON HIGHWAY

Drag each component into the slot where it belongs, or tap a component and then tap a slot. Both routes converge before they reach oxygen.

What you just built

Both routes merge at coenzyme Q. From that point on, every electron takes the same path.

The four complexes and two mobile carriers

  • Complex I (NADH-Q oxidoreductase) — oxidizes NADH, reduces coenzyme Q, pumps protons
  • Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) — the same enzyme as step 6 of the citric acid cycle; passes FADH₂ electrons to Q and pumps nothing
  • Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone) — lipophilic, diffuses inside the membrane, collects from both entry points
  • Complex III (Q-cytochrome c oxidoreductase) — QH₂ to cytochrome c, pumps protons
  • Cytochrome c — small, water-soluble, carries one electron at a time on its heme iron
  • Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) — reduces O₂ to water, pumps protons

A detail worth noticing. Complex IV draws 8 H⁺ out of the matrix for every O₂ it reduces: four are built into two molecules of water, and four are pumped across. Because one O₂ accepts four electrons, that works out to 2 H⁺ pumped per pair of electrons — which is the number in the table.

Module 2

Build the battery

Central question: where is the energy from NADH actually stored?

The electron transport chain makes no ATP. What it makes is a charged membrane — a difference in both proton concentration and electrical charge that the mitochondrion can spend later. If you take one idea from this whole companion, take this one.

By the end of this module you should be able to

  • Describe the pH and charge of the matrix and intermembrane space during active transport
  • Explain why an impermeable inner membrane is a requirement, not a detail
  • Name the two components of the proton motive force
  • Predict what happens to electron transport, the gradient, and ATP output when any one of them is disturbed
Module 2 · Section 1

Charging the mitochondrion

Start from a mitochondrion at rest, with equal proton concentrations on both sides of the inner membrane. Then transfer electrons and watch what separates.

MANIPULATE · CHARGE THE MITOCHONDRION

Move the slider to transfer electron pairs through the chain. Every reading updates live.

Two things are being separated at once, and both store energy:

The proton motive force has two parts

  • A chemical gradient (ΔpH) — matrix around pH 7.8, intermembrane space around pH 7.0
  • An electrical gradient (Δψ) — the intermembrane space is positive relative to the matrix

GO DEEPER · OPTIONAL

pH is a logarithmic scale, so a difference of 0.8 units is a difference of about six-fold in actual [H⁺]. That is worth seeing once, but the number to carry forward is the direction of the gradient, not its magnitude.

None of this works without an impermeable barrier. The inner mitochondrial membrane blocks free diffusion of H⁺, and of ATP and ADP, which is why dedicated transport proteins exist for the latter two. A gradient across a leaky membrane is not a gradient for long — a fact Module 5 exploits.

Module 2 · Section 2

Coupling, and what breaks it

Electron transport and ATP synthesis are two distinct processes joined by one shared resource. Neither touches the other directly. Both depend on the gradient, so interfering with either eventually stops the other.

Module 3

Spend the gradient

Central question: how does proton movement become ATP?

ATP synthase is a rotary motor. Protons flowing through the membrane turn a shaft, and the turning shaft squeezes three catalytic sites through a repeating cycle of shapes. Understanding it as a machine is far easier than memorizing three letters.

By the end of this module you should be able to

  • Assign a function to F₀, F₁, the γ subunit, and the β subunits
  • Order the O, L, and T states and say what happens in each
  • Explain why one 360° rotation yields three ATP
  • Connect roughly 4 H⁺ per ATP to the yields you will use in Module 4
Module 3 · Section 1

Anatomy of the machine

F₀F₁ ATP synthase has a part in the membrane and a part in the matrix, connected by a shaft. Protons never reach the catalytic sites — their energy arrives there as mechanical rotation.

Proton flow turns F₀. F₀ turns γ. γ deforms the β subunits. The β subunits make ATP.

MATCH · WHO DOES WHAT

Assign the correct function to each component.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Module 3 · Section 2

The binding-change cycle

The three β subunits are never in the same state at the same time. The γ shaft is asymmetric, so at any instant one site is open, one is loose, and one is tight. Turning the shaft advances all three at once.

The three states

  • O (open) — low affinity: ATP leaves, ADP and Pᵢ enter
  • L (loose) — ADP and Pᵢ are held but do not react
  • T (tight) — the bond forms and ATP is made

Module 4 · Section 1

Building the ATP ledger

By the time glucose has been through glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, and the citric acid cycle, the cell holds 4 ATP, 10 NADH, and 2 FADH₂. Two of those NADH were made in the cytosol; the other eight were made inside the matrix.

Everything from here is division. Protons pumped, divided by protons spent.

CALCULATE · ATP YIELD LEDGER

Choose the yield for each carrier, then choose a tissue. The total updates as you go.

Where 2.5 and 1.5 come from. 10 H⁺ per NADH ÷ ~4 H⁺ per ATP ≈ 2.5. 6 H⁺ per FADH₂ ÷ ~4 ≈ 1.5. These are ratios, not constants — which is why older textbooks list 3 and 2 instead.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Module 4 · Section 2

Tracing the shuttles

Cytosolic NADH cannot cross the inner membrane. Neither can its electrons, on their own. So the cell moves the electrons using molecules that do have carriers — and the route it picks decides the yield.

The two shuttles differ in one respect that matters: whether the electrons still get to pass through complex I.

EXPLORE · SHUTTLE TRACER

Pick a shuttle, then put its steps in order. When the route is correct, the entry point into the chain is revealed.

WHY THE TISSUE DIFFERENCE

The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle is one-way and fast, which suits tissues with sudden, large energy demands. The malate-aspartate shuttle is reversible and more efficient, which suits tissues doing steady biosynthetic work. Muscle trades ATP for speed; liver keeps the ATP.

Mapping the inhibitors

CLINICAL CONNECTION

Cyanide poisoning presents with normal blood oxygen and tissues that cannot use it. Because complex IV is blocked, cells fall back on glycolysis, and the lactate produced drives a metabolic acidosis. The paradox — adequate oxygen delivery, failing oxygen use — is the diagnostic clue.

Module 5 · Section 2

Uncouplers and drug targets

An inhibitor and an uncoupler both end with less ATP, which is why they are easy to confuse. They are told apart by oxygen consumption, which moves in opposite directions.

Three ways to uncouple

  • 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) — a small acid that ferries protons across the membrane. Sold as a weight-loss drug in the 1930s; withdrawn after fatal hyperthermia.
  • UCP-1 (thermogenin) — a regulated proton channel in brown adipose tissue. Physiological, not toxic: it is how infants and hibernating mammals make heat without shivering.
  • Metformin — not an uncoupler, but a partial complex I inhibitor. Lower hepatic ATP raises AMP, which activates AMPK, which suppresses gluconeogenesis.

Diagnose the mitochondrion

Nothing new is taught here. This lab exists to make the five modules behave as one mental model rather than five separate topics.

You are handed a set of readings from a mitochondrial preparation — electron transport rate, oxygen consumption, proton gradient, ATP output, heat — and asked what happened. That is the reasoning direction exams use, and it is the opposite of the direction lectures teach in.

The three patterns that separate every case

  • Uncoupler: gradient down, oxygen consumption up, ATP down, heat up
  • Chain inhibitor: gradient down, oxygen consumption down, ATP down
  • ATP synthase inhibitor: gradient up, oxygen consumption down, ATP down
