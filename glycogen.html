Module 1

Why glycogen? Structure, branching, and tissue roles

Before any pathway makes sense, one question has to be settled: why does a cell go to the trouble of polymerizing glucose at all? The answer explains the branching, the branching explains the speed, and the tissue differences explain almost everything about regulation later on.

By the end of this module you should be able to

  • Explain the osmotic argument for storing glucose as a polymer.
  • Distinguish α1→4 from α1→6 linkages, and reducing from nonreducing ends.
  • Predict how branch frequency changes the rate of synthesis and degradation.
  • Assign a given property to hepatic or skeletal-muscle glycogen and say why.

Module 1 · Section 2

Bonds, ends, and branching

Glycogen is glucose joined by α1→4 bonds along its linear stretches, with α1→6 bonds at every branch point — roughly one every 8 to 12 residues. A particle holds somewhere between 2,000 and 60,000 residues.

One reducing end, buried at the core. Every branch adds another nonreducing end at the surface — and every nonreducing end is a place an enzyme can work.

Module 3

Glycogenesis: build it up

Synthesis is not degradation run backward. It uses five enzymes, an activated sugar that degradation never touches, and a primer protein that solves a problem most students do not notice until it is pointed out.

Five enzymes. Note that glucose-1-phosphate is shared with degradation, but UDP-glucose belongs to synthesis alone.

Module 4

The glycogen control room

This is the hardest section of the lecture, and a static diagram is not enough for it. The approach here is to give you the controls: set a physiological condition, predict what the two pathways will do, and then check yourself against the dashboard.

Four ideas do most of the work

  • One cascade serves two hormones: GPCR → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylase kinase.
  • The same PKA switches degradation on and synthesis off — reciprocal regulation.
  • Phosphorylation is a tag, not a verdict. Its meaning depends on the protein.
  • Muscle phosphorylase reads energy charge and calcium; liver phosphorylase reads glucose and hormones.
Module 4 · Section 1

The cAMP cascade

Glucagon and epinephrine both act through G protein-coupled receptors, and from the receptor onward the pathway is identical. Each tier amplifies the one above it, which is how a few hormone molecules produce a large metabolic response.

One kinase, two opposite outcomes. PDE acts on the messenger; PP1 acts one tier lower, on the enzymes the cascade already modified.

Put the cascade in order

Module 4 · Section 2

Phosphorylation detective

Most students arrive with a shortcut: phosphorylation means activation. Glycogen metabolism is the cleanest place in biochemistry to break that habit, because one kinase acts on two enzymes in the same response and does opposite things to them.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PKA phosphorylates both phosphorylase kinase and glycogen synthase during a glucagon response. What happens to the two pathways?

Phosphorylation activates glycogen phosphorylase and inactivates glycogen synthase. What does that pairing illustrate?

Module 4 · Section 3

Isoforms and allostery

Glycogen phosphorylase is a homodimer that exists in four interconvertible forms: the covalent state (a, phosphorylated; b, unphosphorylated) crossed with the conformational state (R, more active; T, less active). Allosteric effectors move a molecule between R and T without changing its covalent state at all.

Two isozymes, two jobs. Muscle spends glycogen on itself, so it listens to its own energy charge; the liver spends glycogen on everyone else, so it listens to the blood.

Module 5

When the pathway breaks

Glycogen storage disorders are worth learning as reasoning practice rather than as a list of names. The chain runs in one direction: presentation → tissue → pathway → enzyme. Work it that way and the disease name falls out at the end instead of having to be memorized at the start.

SCOPE FOR THIS COURSE

Five disorders are covered in depth and you should be able to reason your way to each one: Von Gierke (I), Pompe (II), Cori (III), Andersen (IV), and McArdle (V). Three more — Hers (VI), type VII, and type VIII — appear for recognition only, so that the pattern of "which step, how completely blocked" makes sense. You will not be asked to recall their details.

The disease matrix

FOR RECOGNITION ONLY

Three further types round out the pattern. Hers (VI) is a hepatic phosphorylase defect and resembles a mild type I. Type VII is a muscle phosphofructokinase defect and resembles type V. Type VIII is a hepatic phosphorylase kinase defect, milder still, because the hormonal route to phosphorylase is weakened rather than the enzyme removed.
