Module 1

Fundamentals, Catalysis & the Active Site

Before kinetics or inhibition make sense, you need the basics: what an enzyme actually does, why reactions need a push even when they're favorable, why enzymes are so picky about their substrates, and the vocabulary for the helper molecules many enzymes depend on.

Module 1 · Section 1

What enzymes do

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. A reaction that would take years on its own can finish in seconds once the right enzyme is present.

The critical detail is that the enzyme is not used up. It binds its substrate, does its work, releases the product, and is immediately available to run the cycle again. One enzyme molecule can turn over thousands of substrate molecules.

Each enzyme is highly selective. Thousands of enzymes have been identified, and each acts on its own specific substrate or small set of substrates.

Hold on to these

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts — they change the rate of a reaction.
  • They emerge unchanged and are reused.
  • Only a handful of active-site amino acids do the chemistry, and they often come from distant parts of the folded chain — which is why 3-D structure matters so much.

Module 1 · Section 2

The energetics of catalysis

Every reaction climbs through a high-energy transition state before reaching product. The size of that climb is the activation energy. If the molecules don't have enough energy to make the climb, the reaction essentially doesn't happen — no matter how favorable the overall reaction is.

Sucrose burning to carbon dioxide and water makes the point. The reaction releases a great deal of energy, and yet a sugar cube sits on the counter indefinitely without bursting into flame.

Watch what changes — and what stays exactly the same.

The enzyme lowers the barrier by binding and stabilizing the transition state. What it does not do is change ΔG. The same energy is released whether sucrose is broken down by an enzyme or by a flame. An enzyme changes how fast you get there, never where you end up.

Cells handle genuinely unfavorable reactions a different way: by coupling them to a favorable one, most often ATP hydrolysis, so the combined process is favorable overall.

Module 1 · Section 3

Specificity and the active site

Enzymes are remarkably picky. Many catalyze exactly one reaction on exactly one substrate. Because proteins are built from chiral amino acids, the active site is itself chiral — so an enzyme can tell one stereoisomer from another.

Glucokinase makes the point cleanly: it phosphorylates glucose but leaves galactose alone, even though the two sugars differ at a single carbon.

Induced fit accounts for the fact that proteins are dynamic, not frozen.

Binding matters because forming the enzyme-substrate complex is what makes the rate acceleration possible. As the reaction proceeds the active site keeps adjusting — first to grip the substrate, then to stabilize the transition state on the way to product.

CLINICAL CONNECTION Why stereochemistry reaches the pharmacy

The same chirality that lets glucokinase reject galactose is why two enantiomers of a drug can behave like two different drugs. Biological targets discriminate between stereoisomers, so one enantiomer may carry the activity while its mirror image contributes little, or something unwanted.

Module 1 · Section 4

The vocabulary of enzyme helpers

Plenty of enzymes cannot work on protein alone. They need a partner molecule or ion, and the terminology for those partners is precise — and heavily tested.

  • Cofactor — any non-protein helper required for activity. The umbrella term, covering both metal ions and organic molecules.
  • Coenzyme — a cofactor that is an organic molecule.
  • Prosthetic group — a coenzyme permanently, covalently attached to the protein.
  • Apoenzyme — the protein without its required cofactor. Inactive.
  • Holoenzyme — the complete, active enzyme-plus-cofactor complex.
  • Isozymes — different amino acid sequences catalyzing the same reaction. They differ in tissue distribution, kinetic parameters, and sensitivity to regulation.

Alcohol dehydrogenase is the example worth memorizing, because it uses two helpers at once: a Zn²⁺ ion, which is inorganic, and NAD⁺, which is organic. Both are cofactors. Only NAD⁺ is a coenzyme.

Many vitamins earn their keep this way — vitamins B1 through B12, C, and K either serve as cofactors or are the raw material the body uses to build them.

Module 2

Enzyme Kinetics & Factors Affecting Activity

Once you know what an enzyme does, the next question is how fast — and what changes that speed. This module works through the Michaelis-Menten relationship, what Km and kcat each tell you, and how substrate, temperature, and pH all move the needle.

Module 2 · Section 1

Substrate concentration and the Michaelis-Menten curve

Four things set the rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction: substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, temperature, and pH. This module works through all four, starting with substrate.

Add substrate to a fixed amount of enzyme and the rate climbs — but not forever. Eventually every active site is occupied, and adding more substrate changes nothing. That ceiling is Vmax. The whole relationship is captured by one equation:

v0 = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])

Km is the substrate concentration that produces half of Vmax. Read it as a measure of how tightly the enzyme holds its substrate: a lower Km means a stronger interaction, because it takes less substrate to reach half-maximal speed.

Many enzymes have evolved so that their substrate sits near Km under normal cellular conditions. That is not a coincidence. Around Km the curve is steep, so a small rise or fall in substrate produces a meaningful change in rate — the enzyme is responsive. Far above Km the enzyme is saturated and has effectively lost the ability to respond at all.

Module 2 · Section 2

Turnover number and catalytic efficiency

Km tells you about binding. It says nothing about how fast the enzyme works once the substrate is in place. That job belongs to kcat, the turnover number: how many substrate molecules one active site converts to product per second when it is fully saturated.

Neither number alone tells you which of two enzymes is the better catalyst. An enzyme could bind its substrate beautifully and then process it slowly, or grab weakly and work fast. Combining them gives the specificity constant:

kcat / Km

This is the standard measure of catalytic efficiency, because it accounts for both how well the enzyme captures substrate and how quickly it acts on it. A high kcat/Km means the enzyme is good at both — and neither number alone can tell you that.

kcat/Km

Three numbers, three different questions

  • Km — how much substrate does it take to get to half speed? Lower means tighter binding.
  • kcat — how fast does one saturated active site turn over?
  • kcat/Km — how good is this enzyme overall, at realistic substrate levels?

CLINICAL CONNECTION Why kinetics shows up in drug metabolism

How quickly the body clears a drug depends on how much metabolizing enzyme is present, how much drug is present relative to that enzyme's Km, and how fast the enzyme turns over. When drug concentrations climb well above Km, the enzyme approaches saturation and can no longer speed up in response — which is the kinetic reason some drugs behave very differently at high doses.

Module 2 · Section 3

Enzyme concentration, temperature, and pH

Enzyme concentration

More enzyme means more active sites, so more reaction. The cell controls this on two fronts: how much enzyme it makes, and how fast it destroys what it has made. Signaling pathways typically act by changing gene expression, and regulated degradation pathways can be dialed up or down in response to external signals. Both are slower levers than the ones you will meet in Module 4, but they set the baseline everything else operates against.

Temperature

Raising the temperature raises the rate, because molecules move faster and collide more often. That holds only up to a point. Push too far and the protein denatures, the active site loses its shape, and activity collapses. The falling side of the temperature curve is not a slowdown — it is destruction.

pH

Every enzyme has a pH range where it works best, and that range matches where the enzyme actually lives. pH matters because it sets the ionization state of amino acid side chains, and those charges are what bind substrate and perform catalysis.

Reference: the seven classes

Once you can derive a class, this table is what you are deriving toward. Use it to check yourself, not as something to memorize front to back.

ClassWhat happened to the moleculeExamples worth knowing
Oxidoreductase Electrons transferred — an oxidation paired with a reduction Typically uses NAD⁺/NADH or NADP⁺/NADPH; dominates oxidative phosphorylation; many carry a metal cofactor
Transferase A functional group moved from a donor to an acceptor Kinases (phosphoryl from ATP); histone methylation and acetylation; glycosyltransferases
Hydrolase A bond broken using water, which is incorporated into the products Phosphatases (reversing kinases); peptidases; glycoside hydrolases
Lyase A bond broken by elimination, generating a double bond or a ring Histidine decarboxylase (histidine → histamine); adenylate cyclase (ATP → cAMP)
Isomerase Rearranged into an isomer, with nothing added or removed Central to glycolysis; D→L amino acid conversion; mutases relocate a group within one molecule; DNA-unwinding enzymes are drug targets
Ligase Two molecules joined into one, paid for by ATP hydrolysis DNA ligase; aminoacyl tRNA synthetases
Translocase Something moved across a membrane Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 out, 2 in per ATP; ~70% of neuronal ATP use); ATP synthase runs the logic in reverse. Class created in 2018, so older references list only six.

CLINICAL CONNECTION Hydrolase or lyase?

This is the distinction to get right. Both classes break bonds. The question is whether water participated in the cleavage. If water is consumed and appears in the products, it is a hydrolase. If the bond is broken by elimination and a double bond or ring appears, it is a lyase.

Name clues worth recognizing

  • ___ dehydrogenase → oxidoreductase
  • ___ kinase → transferase
  • ___ phosphatase, ___ peptidase → hydrolase
  • ___ synthetase → often a ligase
  • ___ mutase → isomerase

Treat these as recognition cues, not rules. They are reliable often enough to be useful and not so reliable that you should stop reading the reaction.

Module 4

Enzyme Inhibition & Regulation

The most clinically relevant module: how inhibitors change Km and Vmax, how to read that off a graph, the difference between reversible and covalent inhibition, and how cells regulate enzymes beyond inhibition altogether.

Module 4 · Section 1

Competitive and uncompetitive inhibition

An inhibitor is anything that reduces enzyme activity. Where it binds determines what it does to the kinetics — and whether adding more substrate can rescue the reaction.

Competitive inhibitors

A competitive inhibitor binds the active site of the free enzyme, competing directly with substrate for the same space. Because it is a competition, more substrate wins: flood the enzyme with substrate and the inhibitor is displaced. How much substrate it takes depends on the inhibitor concentration, the inhibitor's affinity for the enzyme (Ki), and the substrate's own affinity (Km).

Kinetically, the enzyme now needs more substrate to reach half-maximal velocity, so Km rises. But with enough substrate it still reaches the same ceiling, so Vmax is unchanged.

Uncompetitive inhibitors

An uncompetitive inhibitor binds somewhere other than the active site — an allosteric site — and it binds the enzyme-substrate complex rather than free enzyme. That inverts the logic: the more substrate present, the more [ES] exists, and the more target the inhibitor has. Adding substrate makes it worse, not better. Both Km and Vmax fall.

Module 4 · Section 3

Irreversible and mechanism-based inhibition

Competitive and uncompetitive inhibitors bind reversibly — remove them and activity returns. Irreversible inhibitors do not. They form a covalent bond to the enzyme, usually modifying a key amino acid side chain in the active site, though they can also act at an allosteric site. They generally carry a highly reactive functional group that does the bonding. That enzyme molecule is finished; the cell only recovers activity by synthesizing new protein.

One subtype is worth special attention. Some enzymes work by forming a temporary covalent bond with their substrate as part of normal catalysis. A mechanism-based inhibitor — also called a suicide inhibitor — mimics that substrate closely enough that the enzyme starts processing it, forms the covalent intermediate, and then cannot complete the reaction to break the bond. The enzyme's own catalytic machinery is what traps it.

Module 4 · Section 4

Allosteric control and isozymes

Inhibition is not the only way to change how much an enzyme does. Enzymes typically have multiple domains, and in addition to the substrate binding site there is often a separate regulatory domain. A molecule binding there is an allosteric modulator, and it can push either direction: a positive modulator raises activity, a negative modulator lowers it.

This is how a cell builds control into a pathway. The end product of a pathway can act as a negative modulator on an early enzyme, so the pathway slows itself down as product accumulates. A signal that the pathway is needed can act as a positive modulator on the same enzyme.

Isozymes give the cell a different kind of control. These are enzymes with different amino acid sequences that catalyze the same reaction, and they differ in tissue distribution, in Km and Vmax toward their substrates, and in which allosteric modulators affect them. One tissue can therefore run the same chemistry on a completely different regulatory setting than another tissue.

CLINICAL CONNECTION Enzymes as drug targets

Every mechanism in this module is a drug strategy. Competitive inhibitors are a large share of the drugs you will dispense. Mechanism-based inhibitors like penicillin achieve durable inhibition from a brief exposure, because activity cannot return until new enzyme is made. Allosteric sites offer targets away from a crowded, highly conserved active site. And isozyme differences between tissues are part of why a drug can act at one site while sparing another.
