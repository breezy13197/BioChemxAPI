Module 1

Building blocks & nucleic acid structure

Before any pathway makes sense, the vocabulary has to be automatic. By the end of this module you should be able to look at any abbreviation — dGTP, CMP, uridine — and say exactly what it is made of, and explain why a single missing oxygen changes the whole behavior of the polymer.

Module 1 · Section 1

Nucleobases: purines and pyrimidines

Five bases appear in human nucleic acids, and they come in exactly two structural families. Getting this split automatic pays off later: the two biosynthetic pathways in Modules 2 and 3 are organized around it, and so is the catabolism in Module 5.

Purines — adenine, guanine

Two fused rings, nine numbered positions. Larger, and built by a completely different strategy from the pyrimidines. Both appear in DNA and RNA.

Pyrimidines — cytosine, thymine, uracil

One six-membered ring, six numbered positions. Cytosine appears in both DNA and RNA; thymine is the DNA-specific base and uracil the RNA-specific one.

Thymine and uracil are worth pausing on. Thymine is simply uracil with a methyl group at carbon 5 — nothing else differs. That is why they are interchangeable as adenine's partner, and it is also why making thymine nucleotides needs one extra enzymatic step that Module 4 is largely about.

Module 1 · Section 3

The sugar, and why it matters

DNA and RNA differ in their sugar by exactly one atom. Ribose carries a hydroxyl group at carbon 2 prime; deoxyribose carries a hydrogen there. Every other position — C1 prime holding the base, C3 prime and C5 prime holding the backbone — is identical.

That sounds like a trivial difference. It is not. Before you toggle anything below, commit to a prediction: what would adding a reactive hydroxyl right beside the backbone linkage do to the polymer?

WHY THIS MATTERS

The lability of RNA is the reason RNA-based products need cold chains and careful formulation, while DNA is comparatively forgiving. The same 2 prime hydroxyl that makes RNA fragile is also what lets it fold into the varied shapes that give RNA its catalytic and regulatory roles.

Module 1 · Section 4

Backbone and base pairing

Nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester linkages: a phosphate bridges the 5 prime hydroxyl of one sugar to the 3 prime hydroxyl of the next. Because the two ends of the chain are chemically different, the strand has a direction, and by convention sequences are written 5 prime to 3 prime.

In a duplex, the sugar-phosphate backbones face outward and the bases face each other. Guanine pairs with cytosine using three hydrogen bonds; adenine pairs with thymine (in DNA) or uracil (in RNA) using two. The two strands run antiparallel — one 5 prime to 3 prime, the other 3 prime to 5 prime.

Build a partner strand below. Watch the hydrogen-bond total as you go and notice how much it depends on which bases you are pairing.

Module 1 in five lines

  • Purines (A, G) are two fused rings; pyrimidines (C, T, U) are one ring.
  • Base + sugar = nucleoside. Base + sugar + phosphate(s) = nucleotide.
  • Thymine is uracil plus a methyl group at C5.
  • Ribose has a 2 prime hydroxyl; deoxyribose has a hydrogen there.
  • G-C pairs use three hydrogen bonds; A-T and A-U use two.

Module 2

Build a purine

There is a version of this pathway that is eleven abbreviations in a row, and learning it that way is both miserable and useless. This module takes the opposite order: first work out where every atom in the purine ring physically comes from, and only then meet the reactions that put them there.

Module 2 · Section 1

Where nucleotides come from

A cell has two possible sources of nucleotides, and they are not evenly matched. Dietary nucleotides are broken down in the gut and only about five percent of them reach circulation. The sugar released can be used for energy; the base cannot. So de novo synthesis — building nucleotides from small precursors — carries essentially the whole load.

Both de novo pathways begin at the same molecule. PRPP synthetase transfers a pyrophosphate group from ATP onto ribose-5-phosphate to make PRPP, and the ribose-5-phosphate itself comes from the pentose phosphate pathway. That is the connection worth holding onto: the pathway usually introduced for NADPH production is also the front door to every nucleotide in the cell.

Module 2 · Section 3

PRPP to IMP: the pathway

Now the reactions. The purine ring is built onto PRPP, which means every intermediate from the first committed step onward is already a nucleotide — there is never a free purine base floating around waiting to be attached.

The step worth naming is the first one. Amidophosphoribosyltransferase converts PRPP to 5-phosphoribosyl-1-amine, and this is the committed step: PRPP has other possible destinations, but once the amine is installed, the molecule is going to become a purine nucleotide. Committed steps are where regulation lives, in this pathway and in every other one.

Put the milestones in order below. You do not need every abbreviation — you need the shape of the route and the two or three places where something chemically interesting happens.

Where the energy goes

ATP is consumed at several steps, including glycine addition and the aspartate condensation. Building a ring from scratch is expensive, which is part of why salvage exists at all.

Where some comes back

Adenylosuccinate lyase releases fumarate, which enters the citric acid cycle. The pathway partially pays for itself.

Module 2 · Section 4

The IMP branch point

IMP is not a nucleotide that ends up in DNA or RNA. It is the fork in the road: two further steps lead to AMP, two different ones lead to GMP.

The interesting part is what powers each branch. Making AMP costs a GTP. Making GMP costs an ATP. Each branch is paid for with the product of the other one.

Rather than memorizing that, run it. Make some AMP, make some GMP, and watch what happens to the two pools.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Module 2 in five lines

  • De novo synthesis supplies most nucleotides; diet supplies very little.
  • PRPP, made from ribose-5-phosphate and ATP, starts both pathways.
  • The purine ring is built directly onto PRPP, atom by atom, from five donors.
  • PRPP to 5-phosphoribosyl-1-amine is the committed step.
  • IMP branches to AMP (using GTP) and GMP (using ATP), which keeps the pools balanced.

Module 3

Same goal, different strategy

Both pathways end at a nucleotide and both need PRPP. Almost everything else about how they get there is different, and the comparison itself is the thing worth learning here — not two separate lists.

Module 3 · Section 1

Two construction strategies

Imagine you have to build a nucleotide from scratch. You have a ribose phosphate available and a collection of small molecules. There are two reasonable approaches: build the ring onto the sugar you already have, or build the ring separately and attach the sugar at the end.

The cell uses both — one for each class of base.

Module 3 · Section 2

CPS II, CPS I, and CAD

Pyrimidine synthesis starts by turning bicarbonate into carbamoyl phosphate, catalyzed by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II. There is also a CPS I, it does a chemically similar reaction, and it belongs to a completely different pathway. Students mix them up constantly, so it is worth fixing the distinction now.

CPS ICPS II
PathwayUrea cyclePyrimidine synthesis
Nitrogen sourceFree ammoniumGlutamine side chain amide

CAD is one polypeptide chain carrying three consecutive enzymatic activities: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II, aspartate transcarbamoylase, and dihydroorotase. Think of it as three workstations on one bench — the intermediate never has to travel far to reach the next reaction. You met the same idea with the bifunctional AIR carboxylase / SAICAR synthetase protein in Module 2, and you will meet it again with UMP synthase in the next section.

Module 3 · Section 3

Orotate to UMP

After CAD, dihydroorotate is oxidized to orotate by dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH). This enzyme is the odd one out in the pathway: it sits in the mitochondrion rather than the cytosol, and it hands its electrons to ubiquinone rather than to NAD or NADP. That ties pyrimidine synthesis directly to the electron transport chain.

Remember DHODH. It becomes a drug target in Module 5, and the reason it is a good one traces back to exactly these two unusual features.

The ring is now finished, and only then does the sugar arrive. Orotate phosphoribosyltransferase attaches PRPP to give OMP; OMP decarboxylase removes a carboxyl group to give UMP. Both activities sit on one polypeptide called UMP synthase — the same multifunctional design as CAD.

Module 3 · Section 4

Climbing the phosphate ladder

Both pathways deliver a monophosphate nucleotide, but nucleic acid synthesis needs triphosphates. The climb has a simple structure worth knowing: each nucleotide has its own monophosphate kinase, but one shared enzyme — nucleoside diphosphate kinase — handles every diphosphate-to-triphosphate step. ATP is the phosphate donor at both levels.

Cytosine nucleotides are a special case. There is no CMP produced by the pyrimidine pathway. Instead, UMP is phosphorylated all the way to UTP, and CTP synthase converts UTP to CTP using the glutamine amide as its nitrogen source.

Run the ladder yourself, choosing the enzyme at each rung.

Module 3 in five lines

  • Pyrimidines build the ring first and attach PRPP afterward — the reverse of purines.
  • CPS II starts the pathway using glutamine nitrogen; CPS I serves the urea cycle using ammonium.
  • CAD carries three consecutive activities on one polypeptide chain.
  • DHODH is mitochondrial and passes electrons to ubiquinone.
  • One shared kinase makes every triphosphate; CTP synthase converts UTP to CTP.

Module 4

Making DNA building blocks

Everything so far has produced ribonucleotides. DNA needs deoxyribonucleotides, and it needs thymine, which no pathway you have seen produces. This module is about how the cell closes both gaps — and it is where the pharmacology in Module 5 comes from.

Module 4 · Section 1

Ribonucleotide reductase and the electron relay

Your cell has UDP. It needs dUDP. Making that happen means removing an oxygen from C2 prime and putting a hydrogen there instead — a gain of electrons at that carbon, which is a reduction. So the first question is not which enzyme does it. The first question is: where do the electrons come from?

Four components are involved. Arrange them so the electrons flow in the right direction.

Note where in the phosphorylation ladder this happens. Ribonucleotide reductase acts on diphosphate nucleotides, so the deoxy conversion is a single defined step: NDP becomes dNDP, and phosphorylation to the triphosphate level happens afterward by the same shared kinase from Module 3.

Module 4 · Section 2

The thymidine problem

Ribonucleotide reductase gives the cell dADP, dGDP, dCDP, and dUDP. Three of those are DNA precursors. The fourth is a problem: DNA does not use uracil, it uses thymine.

Look back at Module 1 for why this is solvable. Thymine is uracil plus a methyl group at C5. So the cell does not need a new pathway — it needs one methyl transfer.

Thymidylate synthase performs it, converting dUMP to dTMP, and it takes the carbon from a tetrahydrofolate derivative. The folate leaves in a more oxidized state and has to be regenerated by dihydrofolate reductase before it can be used again.

That regeneration requirement is the single most pharmacologically important fact in this companion. Build the cycle below and you will see why two different drugs can produce the same shortage.

Module 4 · Section 3

Why the route runs down and back up

Put the two previous sections together and something slightly odd appears. Ribonucleotide reductase works at the diphosphate level. Thymidylate synthase works at the monophosphate level. DNA polymerase needs triphosphates.

So the journey from UDP to dTTP goes down the ladder and then back up it. Assemble that route below, and the reason for the dip should become obvious as you place each piece.

Module 4 in five lines

  • Ribonucleotide reductase converts NDP to dNDP by replacing the 2 prime hydroxyl with hydrogen.
  • Electrons flow NADPH → thioredoxin reductase → thioredoxin → ribonucleotide reductase.
  • Thymidylate synthase converts dUMP to dTMP using a folate-derived one-carbon unit.
  • Dihydrofolate reductase regenerates the folate so the reaction can run again.
  • The route to dTTP dips to the monophosphate level and then climbs back up.

Module 5

Break the pathway

Four modules of construction. This one is about what happens when you take a piece out — deliberately, with a drug, or unavoidably, in disease. The order here is intentional: you predict the biochemical consequence first, and only then find out which drug does it.

Module 5 · Section 1

Perturbation lab: predict, then meet the drug

The usual way to learn this material is a list: drug X inhibits enzyme Y. That list is easy to memorize and almost useless, because an exam question rarely asks for the pairing directly — it asks what happens downstream.

So this runs the other way. Each round below removes an enzyme from the pathway you have spent four modules building. Predict the consequence. Then the drug appears.

THE COMMON THREAD

All three drugs exploit the same vulnerability: a cell that is dividing quickly needs a continuous supply of nucleotides, and a cell that is not dividing does not. That is the whole basis of their selectivity — and also the reason their side effects land on other fast-turnover tissue such as bone marrow, gut lining, and hair follicles.

Module 5 · Section 2

Taking nucleotides apart

Catabolism of both classes opens the same way: strip the phosphate, remove the sugar, then degrade the base. The released sugar has real value — it can be recycled or oxidized for energy. The base does not, which is exactly why dietary nucleotides made such a poor energy source back in Module 2.

After that shared opening, the two classes separate completely.
