    Module 1

    Energy economy & fuel storage

    Three questions organize this module: what fuels are available, how efficiently each can be stored, and what determines how much energy the body spends. The deeper lesson is why the body stores fuels so differently.

    Module 1 · Section 1

    What fuels are available?

    The typical diet supplies energy as carbohydrate, fat, protein, and sometimes alcohol. On a food label, a “Calorie” is actually a kilocalorie (kcal): the energy needed to raise 1 liter of water by 1 °C.

    Module 1 · Section 2

    Build the fuel warehouse

    Only fat and carbohydrate have dedicated storage forms. Most stored energy sits in adipose triglyceride, and the reason is packaging. Triglyceride is hydrophobic, so adipose tissue is only about 15% water. Glycogen is hydrophilic and holds roughly 4 g of water for every gram of glycogen, on top of yielding less than half the energy per gram.

    Module 1 · Section 3

    Fuel storage sort

    Not every fuel molecule in the body is “stored” in the same sense. Some sit in dedicated reserves built for withdrawal. Some are working tissue that can be broken down in an emergency, at a cost. And some are simply fuel in transit between the tissue that releases it and the tissue that burns it.

    Dedicated storage

    Built to be filled and emptied: glycogen in liver and muscle, triglyceride in adipose.

    Functional tissue that can be mobilized

    Protein. There is no protein reserve, so every gram burned is a gram of enzyme, transporter, or muscle fiber lost.

    Module 1 · Section 4

    The DEE mixer

    Daily energy expenditure (DEE) has three parts. Basal metabolic rate is the cost of staying alive at rest: running organs, maintaining electrochemical gradients, and keeping pathways turning. Physical activity is ATP spent on muscle contraction. Diet-induced thermogenesis is the cost of digesting, absorbing, distributing, and storing food, about 10% of the total.

    Module 2

    The metabolic state simulator

    Instead of memorizing fed, postabsorptive, and starvation states as three lists, follow one person through time after a meal. The same six questions apply at every stage: blood glucose, the insulin/glucagon ratio, and what the liver, adipose, muscle, and brain are doing.

    Module 2 · Section 1 · 0–4 hours

    After a meal: the fed state

    Each macronutrient takes a different road out of the intestine, and the road decides which organ sees it first.

    Glucose and amino acids

    Absorbed into the hepatic portal vein and delivered to the liver first. Glucose then reaches the pancreas and raises insulin, which drives uptake into muscle, adipose, and liver and storage as glycogen. Amino acids build proteins; surplus is oxidized or, without carbohydrate, used for glucose.

    Triglyceride

    Packaged with protein, phospholipid, and cholesterol into chylomicrons that enter the lymph and bypass the liver. Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) on capillary walls releases fatty acids to adipose and muscle, and insulin promotes their storage in adipose.

    Module 2 · Section 2 · ~12 hours

    The overnight fast

    Twelve hours after eating, insulin is low and glucagon is high. The liver now maintains blood glucose, first by breaking down glycogen and then, increasingly, by gluconeogenesis from three carbon sources: glycerol from triglyceride, lactate from glycolysis, and amino acids, predominantly from muscle. Ketone body production begins but is not yet a major fuel, and urea production rises as amino acids are metabolized.

      Module 2 · Section 3 · days to weeks

      Prolonged starvation

      As a fast stretches into days, the brain increasingly uses ketone bodies and total glucose requirements fall, so hepatic glucose output levels off. Red blood cells still need glucose, but their lactate is recycled. Fatty acid oxidation feeds ketone body synthesis, and citric acid cycle flux drops because acetyl-CoA is diverted to ketone bodies and oxaloacetate to glucose.

      Module 3

      Hormones as a regulatory network

      Memorizing seven hormones one at a time tends to fall apart the moment two of them act together. This module asks a different question: given a signal, what should every tissue do? And given a situation, which signals should rise or fall?

      Module 3 · Section 1

      Insulin & glucagon: the master switch

      Insulin is released when blood glucose rises after a meal. It is anabolic: it tells liver and muscle to take up glucose and amino acids and adipose to take up fatty acids and glucose, then build glycogen, protein, and triglyceride. In the liver it also stimulates fatty acid and VLDL synthesis while inhibiting gluconeogenesis and glycogen breakdown; in adipose it inhibits triglyceride hydrolysis.

      Glucagon is released when glucose falls between meals. It mobilizes fuel in the liver, stimulating glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis while inhibiting glycogen synthesis and the conversion of glucose to fatty acids. Skeletal muscle has no glucagon receptors, so muscle does not respond to it directly.

      Module 3 · Section 2

      Hormone control panel

      Beyond insulin and glucagon, four more signals reshape fuel use. Growth hormone works directly and through IGF-I and mainly increases fatty acid availability. Catecholamines mobilize fuel and push the pancreas toward glucagon. Glucocorticoids raise glucose by driving gluconeogenesis and limiting peripheral uptake. Thyroid hormones raise BMR and oxygen use and sensitize tissues to catecholamines.

      Module 3 · Section 4 · Pharmacotherapy connection

      Incretins & the drugs built on them

      Some metabolic hormones come from outside the classic endocrine glands. The small intestine releases GLP-1 and GIP, the incretins, which amplify insulin secretion after a meal. It also releases GLP-2, which is intestinotrophic: it increases mucosal proliferation and gut barrier function. GLP-1 and GLP-2 come from proglucagon, the same precursor as glucagon; the intestine and pancreas simply process it differently.

      Module 4

      The metabolic division of labor

      Different organs solve different metabolic problems. The liver directs traffic, adipose warehouses, muscle meets demand that swings from rest to sprint, the heart never stops, the brain is picky, and red blood cells have no choice at all. Seeing why each organ burns what it burns is what makes fasting physiology predictable.

      Module 4 · Section 2

      The liver: metabolic traffic controller

      Most nutrients absorbed from the gut pass through the liver before reaching the rest of the body, because the hepatic portal vein drains the GI tissues straight to it. Long-chain fatty acids are the exception, bypassing it through the lymph. That position makes the liver the first to see every meal, and every swallowed drug.

      What the liver manages

      • Blood glucose: glycogen storage when high, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis when low
      • Lipid synthesis and export of triglyceride and cholesterol
      • Amino acid metabolism and the urea cycle
      • Ketone body production when fatty acid oxidation is high
      • Fructose and galactose metabolism
      • Xenobiotic inactivation, most notably by cytochrome P450 enzymes

      Clinical connection

      The liver accounts for roughly 20% of total oxygen use. It can oxidize essentially every major fuel except ketone bodies. For pharmacy, its position on the portal vein explains first-pass metabolism: an oral dose may be substantially metabolized by CYP450 enzymes before it ever reaches systemic circulation.

      Module 4 · Section 3

      Muscle energy timeline

      Cardiac muscle is a continuous oxidative consumer, running mainly on fatty acids (about 60–70%) with lactate and glucose. Skeletal muscle is a variable-demand consumer: ATP is the immediate energy source, phosphocreatine rapidly regenerates it, glycolysis takes over as seconds become minutes, and fatty acids dominate as exercise lengthens.

        Module 4 · Section 4 · Clinical connection

        Creatine to creatinine

        Creatine synthesis begins in the kidney and finishes in the liver, using glycine, arginine, and methionine (as the methyl donor SAM). Muscle takes up creatine, and creatine kinase phosphorylates it to phosphocreatine, a more efficient way to store high-energy phosphate than ATP itself. Creatine and phosphocreatine then cyclize spontaneously into creatinine at a steady rate, and the kidney excretes it. That steady input is why serum creatinine is used to estimate glomerular filtration rate (GFR).

        Redrawn from the lecture’s phosphocreatine and creatinine slides. Creatine kinase runs both ways: charging phosphocreatine at rest, regenerating ATP during sudden demand.
