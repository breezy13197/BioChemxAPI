Module 1

Membrane Transport: predict the direction

Only small, neutral, lipophilic molecules slip through a lipid bilayer on their own. Everything else — ions, sugars, polar molecules — needs a protein. This module is about which protein, moving which way, and paid for by what.

Every section follows the same loop: read a short explanation, predict what will happen, run it and watch, then answer a checkpoint that asks you to explain the result.

Module 1 · Section 1

Passive transport

The interior of a membrane is a wall of hydrocarbon tails. A small neutral lipophilic molecule dissolves into it and diffuses straight through. An ion cannot: it carries charge and drags a shell of water with it, and stripping that shell costs more energy than the bilayer will ever give back.

So ions and polar molecules use membrane transport proteins. When the molecule moves down its own gradient — high concentration to low — the process is passive. No energy is added; the second law does the work. A carrier protein simply changes shape to shuttle the solute across, and a channel simply opens a hole.

Gating decides when a channel is open, never which way the solute goes. Ligand-gated channels open when a signaling molecule binds — the usual arrangement at a synapse. Voltage-gated channels open when the membrane potential changes. Both are still passive once open.

Three routes across a bilayer. Only the first needs no protein — and all three, as drawn, are moving solute down its gradient.

Module 1 · Section 2

Primary active transport

Sometimes a cell needs a solute moved the wrong way — low concentration to high. That is thermodynamically uphill (ΔG > 0), so it will not happen on its own. Active transport couples the uphill movement to an energy source. In primary active transport that energy is applied directly to the transporter, almost always as ATP hydrolysis.

The reference example is the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. One ATP hydrolyzed moves 3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ in, both against their gradients. Because three positive charges leave for every two that enter, the pump is also electrogenic — it contributes to the negative resting membrane potential.

That work is expensive. In excitable cells, roughly 75% of total energy expenditure goes to this one pump, because every action potential dissipates a little of the gradients the pump then has to rebuild.

One ATP, three sodium out, two potassium in — and both ions moving uphill.

Module 1 · Section 3

Secondary active transport & transporter types

A gradient is stored energy. Once the Na⁺/K⁺ pump has built a steep inward Na⁺ gradient, other proteins can spend it. In secondary active transport — also called cotransport — one solute is allowed to run downhill and the free energy released drags a second solute uphill. No ATP touches the transporter itself.

SGLT1 and SGLT2 do exactly this: Na⁺ enters with its gradient, and glucose is pulled in against its own. Both move in the same direction, which makes SGLT a symporter.

Two independent labels describe any transporter. How many substrates: a uniporter carries one; symporters and antiporters are cotransporters carrying two. And which direction: same for symport, opposite for antiport. A uniporter can be passive or active — the substrate count says nothing about the energetics.

Substrate count and direction are separate questions. Answer both and the transporter names itself.

Module 2

GPCR signaling: build the cascade

A signal transduction pathway is a relay. A signal arrives outside, a receptor converts it into an event inside, a second messenger spreads and amplifies it, and a kinase converts it into a response. G-protein coupled receptors are the largest family that works this way — around 34% of approved drugs act on one.

You'll build the β-adrenergic cascade card by card, watch the amplification, and then take the same receptor down a different branch.

Module 2 · Section 1

Anatomy of a signal transduction pathway

Nearly every pathway in this lecture has the same five parts. A signal is released in response to some stimulus — stress, hunger, injury. It binds a receptor, usually in the plasma membrane, and changes that receptor's conformation. The receptor engages a transducer inside the cell, which switches on an effector enzyme that generates a second messenger. The messenger activates a kinase, and the kinase produces the response.

The messenger step is where amplification happens: one activated receptor can generate tens to hundreds of second messenger molecules, because the enzyme making them turns over repeatedly.

A GPCR has three structural regions: an extracellular N-terminal ligand-binding domain, seven transmembrane α-helices (hence "7-TM" or heptahelical), and an intracellular C-terminal domain where the G protein docks. Histamine, opioid, adrenergic, dopamine, serotonin, and cannabinoid receptors are all GPCRs.

Seven helices through the bilayer, ligand above, transducer below. Every GPCR in the course shares this plan.

Module 2 · Section 2

The β-adrenergic cascade

Epinephrine is released by the adrenal glands under stress. It binds the β-adrenergic receptor, which activates the heterotrimeric G protein sitting on the inner face of the membrane. The Gα subunit exchanges GDP for GTP, dissociates from the complex, and activates adenylate cyclase.

Adenylate cyclase converts ATP into cyclic AMP. This is the amplification step: one activated cyclase produces many cAMP molecules. cAMP then activates protein kinase A, and PKA produces the response — which depends entirely on the tissue. In cardiac muscle, increased rate and ATP production; in the stomach, increased acid secretion.

Hold on to the switch logic, because you will meet it again in Module 3: GTP bound means on; GDP bound means off.

Module 2 · Section 3

Same beginning, different messenger

Not every GPCR runs through adenylate cyclase. Some couple to a G protein that activates phospholipase C instead, and everything downstream changes.

PLC cleaves PIP₂ — phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate — into two products with very different chemistry. IP₃ is the soluble head group; it diffuses through the cytosol and triggers release of Ca²⁺ from intracellular stores. DAG keeps its fatty acyl tails and cannot leave the membrane. Ca²⁺ and DAG together activate protein kinase C, and Ca²⁺ also activates other proteins on its own.

The branch point is the effector enzyme, not the receptor architecture. Both branches start with a 7-TM receptor and a G protein.

Module 3

Receptor tyrosine kinases: insulin and EGFR

These two pathways use nearly the same vocabulary — receptor, autophosphorylation, adaptor proteins, kinase cascade, response — which is exactly why they get confused. This module builds them separately, then puts them side by side on purpose.

Module 3 · Section 1

The insulin receptor pathway

Insulin is a peptide hormone: two chains held together by three disulfide bonds. Its receptor is a membrane-bound dimer — already assembled before insulin arrives. Each monomer has an extracellular α subunit that binds insulin and an intracellular β subunit carrying a protein kinase domain. That makes it a receptor tyrosine kinase.

Insulin binding to the α subunits brings the β subunits together, and each one phosphorylates tyrosine residues on the other — autophosphorylation. Those new phosphotyrosines recruit and phosphorylate IRS1/2, which activate PI3K. PI3K is a lipid kinase: it phosphorylates PIP₂ to make PIP₃, the second messenger, which kicks off a short cascade ending at Akt (protein kinase B).

PIP₃ and IP₃ are different molecules and different pathways. Same starting material, different enzyme, different fate.

A receptor that is already a dimer, activated by phosphorylating itself.

Module 3 · Section 2

The EGFR pathway

Epidermal growth factor is a small extracellular protein that drives growth of epidermal and epithelial cells. Its receptor, EGFR, is also a receptor tyrosine kinase — but unlike the insulin receptor it is not a dimer to begin with. Each monomer binds its own EGF molecule, and only then do the two associate. That conformational change brings the intracellular kinase domains close enough to autophosphorylate each other.

The new phosphotyrosines recruit Grb2, which recruits Sos. Sos is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor: it makes Ras swap GDP for GTP. Ras is a small G protein, but it obeys the same rule as Gα — GTP on, GDP off.

GTP-bound Ras activates Raf at the membrane, Raf phosphorylates MEK, MEK phosphorylates ERK, and ERK travels to the nucleus to phosphorylate transcription factors. ERK belongs to the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family.

A phosphorylation relay with a GTP switch bolted into the middle of it.

Module 3 · Section 3

Three pathways, side by side

You now have all three. Most exam confusion comes from the parts they share, so it is worth writing out where they actually diverge: which receptor class, whether a G protein is involved and which kind, what the second messenger is, and which kinase ends the cascade.

Two contrasts to keep close at hand. Heterotrimeric versus small G protein: the β-adrenergic pathway uses Gα out of a three-subunit complex; EGFR uses monomeric Ras. Both switch on by binding GTP and off by hydrolyzing it. And IP₃ versus PIP₃: same PIP₂ precursor, but PLC cleaves it and PI3K phosphorylates it.

Module 4

Turn it off: termination, and what happens when it fails

Every pathway you have built has an off switch, and the off switch is not an afterthought. A pathway stuck in the ON position is the biochemical definition of a large fraction of human cancer. This module works through the three termination mechanisms, then deliberately breaks them.

Module 4 · Section 1

Three ways to end a signal

The simplest termination is the signal itself going away: once the ligand is no longer produced it dissociates, and the receptor reverts to its inactive form. Beyond that, three biochemical mechanisms do the real work.

GTP hydrolysis. G proteins have intrinsic GTPase activity. Gα slowly hydrolyzes its bound GTP to GDP; the GDP-bound subunit releases adenylate cyclase and rejoins the heterotrimer. The rate matters: too fast and the cascade never runs, too slow and cAMP accumulates unchecked. Ras carries the same built-in timer.

Phosphatases. Pathways built by adding phosphates come apart by removing them. Lipid phosphatases turn PIP₃ back into PIP₂, protein tyrosine phosphatases strip the receptor, and serine phosphatases reverse the activated kinases.

Phosphodiesterases. PDEs hydrolyze cAMP to AMP, which cannot activate PKA. They are common drug targets — caffeine and sildenafil both inhibit PDEs.

Module 4 · Section 2

Break the pathway

Now run the logic backwards. If you know what each component does, you can predict what happens when one of them stops working — and that prediction is what most application questions are actually testing.

The clinical payoff is EGFR. Its output is proliferation, so a stuck ON switch translates directly into tumor growth. Almost every gene in the pathway is a proto-oncogene. The Ras family is the most commonly mutated protein family in human cancer, with K-Ras the most prevalent — permanently activated K-Ras appears in roughly 90% of pancreatic cancers. Eleven approved drugs target EGFR directly, and K-Ras itself, long considered undruggable, finally yielded to sotorasib, approved in 2021 for non-small cell lung cancer.

Note what this implies for treatment: blocking EGF from binding EGFR does very little if the break is downstream at Ras.
