Globin Foundations, Heme & Oxygen-Carrying Capacity

Two proteins, one piece of chemistry. Hemoglobin and myoglobin both use the same iron-containing group to bind oxygen, but they are built very differently — and almost everything that follows in this companion comes from that difference in structure.

Module 1 · Section 1

Hemoglobin and myoglobin

Module 1 · Section 2

Heme, prosthetic groups and iron

In both proteins the part that actually binds oxygen is heme b, an iron-containing porphyrin. The protein itself does not touch the oxygen — the iron atom at the centre of the heme does.

Heme is covalently attached to the globin chain, which makes it a prosthetic group: a non-amino-acid component tightly or covalently bound to a protein. And because both proteins contain iron, they belong to the wider class of metalloproteins — an estimated 50% of all proteins are bound to a metal ion.

Where heme comes from

Heme is synthesised mainly in the mitochondria of liver and bone marrow cells. Glycine and acetate supply the nitrogen and carbon atoms of the scaffold, and once that scaffold is built, ferrochelatase inserts the iron atom into the centre.

Iron has two relevant oxidation states, Fe2+ (ferrous) and Fe3+ (ferric). Only Fe2+ binds oxygen within hemoglobin. Hold onto that — Module 5 is largely about what goes wrong when iron ends up stuck as Fe3+.

Module 2 · Section 1

Six coordination sites

The Fe2+ atom coordinates six groups total — but they don't all come from the same source, and not everything in the tray below belongs in this diagram at all. Build the coordination environment yourself before reading the explanation underneath it.

Fe2+

How it actually works

Four positions are held by the heme ring itself. A fifth is the imidazole side chain of the proximal histidine, on an alpha helix near the interface between the two αβ dimers. The sixth is left open for O2.

Distal histidine and 2,3-BPG were in the tray on purpose — neither belongs in this diagram. The distal histidine sits near the pocket and stabilises O2 once it's bound, but it never coordinates the iron itself (Section 2 covers exactly what it does instead). 2,3-BPG doesn't come near the heme pocket at all: it binds a completely different, positively charged site in the central cavity between the β chains (Module 4). If you placed either one in a slot, that's worth remembering — it's a genuinely common mix-up.

When oxygen binds at the sixth site, the heme changes shape and the iron atom moves into the plane of the porphyrin. Because the iron is still bonded to the proximal histidine, the histidine gets pulled along with it — and that is the whole mechanism of communication that Module 3 picks up from here.

Module 2 · Section 2

Redox, the distal histidine and CO

Oxygen binding is not just a docking event, it is a redox event. As O2 binds, the iron is temporarily oxidised from Fe2+ to Fe3+. Something has to be reduced in exchange, and that something is the oxygen itself, which becomes the superoxide anion, O2−. Once the oxygen is released, both changes reverse.

There is a second histidine near the heme pocket: the distal histidine. It does two jobs. It stabilises the bound oxygen molecule, and it makes the pocket less welcoming to other small molecules such as carbon monoxide and isocyanates.

Pharmacy connection

"Less favourable" is not the same as "prevented." Even with the distal histidine discouraging it, CO still binds heme about 250 times more strongly than oxygen does. Structural protection buys margin, not immunity — which is why carbon monoxide exposure remains a genuine emergency.

Module 3

Cooperativity & Quaternary Structure

This is the conceptual centre of the lecture. Four subunits that talk to each other behave completely differently from four subunits working alone, and the difference is measured in how much oxygen actually reaches tissue.

Module 3 · Section 1

Two dimers, four subunits

Hemoglobin is a heterotetramer — two α chains and two β chains — but functionally it behaves as two αβ dimers. When oxygen binds to one of the dimers, the two dimers rotate with respect to one another. The oxygenated form is less conformationally rigid than the deoxygenated form.

The individual heme groups are far too far apart to influence each other directly. All of the communication travels through changes in quaternary structure.

  • If one subunit binds O2, the affinity of the others rises
  • If one subunit releases O2, the affinity of the others falls

Myoglobin saturates early and holds on. Hemoglobin's S-shaped curve is cooperativity made visible — it loads steeply where oxygen is plentiful and unloads steeply where it is not.

Why it matters

Under similar conditions, hemoglobin uses roughly 66% of its oxygen capacity while myoglobin uses roughly 7%. Hemoglobin delivers nearly ten times as much oxygen — using exactly the same heme chemistry.

Module 3 · Section 3

Concerted vs sequential

Two models have been proposed to explain how the subunits move between states as oxygen binds.

Concerted modelSequential model
All four subunits share the same state. The equilibrium shifts toward R as oxygen binding increases. Each subunit changes state independently. Binding induces an intermediate state that is neither T nor R; the subunit fully converts to R once it binds oxygen.

So which one is right? As the lecture puts it, the answer lies somewhere in the middle — and there is evidence on both sides. Assign each observation to the model it supports.

Module 4

Controlling Oxygen Delivery

Cooperativity alone is not enough. If oxyhemoglobin simply stayed in the R state it would never release its oxygen under physiological conditions. This module is about the molecules that push the equilibrium back.

Module 4 · Section 1

2,3-bisphosphoglycerate

2,3-BPG is a side product of glycolysis, which neatly ties the production of ATP from sugar to the consumption of oxygen. It is present at roughly the same concentration as hemoglobin itself.

It is an allosteric effector: allosteric means it binds at a site other than the one used by the native ligand. 2,3-BPG never touches the heme iron. It works from a separate pocket, and it works in one direction — it drives hemoglobin from R back to T, releasing oxygen.

  • 2,3-BPG binds hemoglobin only in the T state
  • Stabilising T makes the R state harder to reach
  • Full oxygen loading then requires high oxygen concentrations — which is exactly what the lungs provide
  • Without 2,3-BPG, hemoglobin would release only about 8% of its bound oxygen to tissue

The pocket it binds is positively charged, and 2,3-BPG is highly negatively charged. Keep that in mind — Module 5 shows what happens when one of those positive residues is swapped for a neutral one.

Module 4 · Section 2

The Bohr effect

Metabolically active tissue generates H+ and CO2. Both stimulate hemoglobin to release oxygen — the Bohr effect. Affinity is directly correlated with pH: as pH falls, so does hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen.

The mechanism is concrete. As pH drops, the imidazole side chain of a histidine residue in the β chain becomes protonated. In deoxyhemoglobin, that protonated histidine can form a salt bridge with an aspartate residue in the same chain, and that bridge stabilises the T state.

A rightward shift means lower affinity at any given oxygen level — more oxygen handed over to the tissue that made the acid.

Module 4 · Section 3

Two routes for CO2

Two mechanisms have been proposed for how CO2 stimulates oxygen release. They are worth keeping separate in your head, because they reach the same destination by different roads.

Indirect — CO2 as a mild acidDirect — carbamate formation
CO2 + H2O → carbonic acid (via carbonic anhydrase) → H+ → protonation → salt bridge → T state stabilised CO2 + N-terminal amino groups → negatively charged carbamates → salt bridges with the other αβ dimer → T state stabilised

Pharmacy connection

Carbamate formation is not only a signal, it is also transport. It accounts for about 15% of total CO2 carried back to the lungs. The remainder travels as bicarbonate, and carbonic anhydrase catalyses the conversion back to CO2 in the lungs.

Module 5

Hemoglobin in Clinical Pharmacy

Three short cases. Each one is the same reasoning exercise: a change in structure, an effect on an interaction, a shift in conformation, and a clinical consequence.

Module 5 · Case 1

Fetal hemoglobin

For oxygen to move from maternal blood into fetal blood, fetal hemoglobin has to hold oxygen more tightly than the mother's. It manages that without changing a single thing about the heme chemistry.

Adult hemoglobin is α2β2. Fetal hemoglobin is α2γ2. The γ chain is about 72% identical to the β chain in primary sequence, and the difference that matters is a single substitution in the 2,3-BPG binding site: a histidine is replaced by a serine.

  • β → γ chain substitution
  • Histidine (positive) → serine (neutral) in the 2,3-BPG pocket
  • Less positive charge → weaker binding of negatively charged 2,3-BPG
  • Less T-state stabilisation → higher oxygen affinity
  • Oxygen transfers from maternal to fetal red blood cells

Module 5 · Case 2

Methemoglobinemia

Module 1 established that only Fe2+ binds oxygen. When the heme iron sits in the Fe3+ state, the protein is called methemoglobin, and it cannot carry oxygen. About 1–2% of circulating hemoglobin is methemoglobin in a healthy person.

Cytochrome-b5 reductase (methemoglobin reductase) converts methemoglobin back to functional hemoglobin. A deficiency in this enzyme predisposes to methemoglobinemia, and certain medications and environmental exposures can cause it as well.

Pharmacy connection

Methemoglobinemia is treated with methylene blue, which helps regenerate NAD+ for cytochrome-b5 reductase. Note that it supports the enzyme pathway rather than acting on hemoglobin directly.

Beyond this lecture: dosing, monitoring and contraindications for methylene blue are clinical pharmacy content and are not covered by the source lecture. Treat them as a separate resource rather than examinable material from this module.

Why the second patient is different

Methylene blue does not reduce Fe3+ by itself. It is first converted to leucomethylene blue, and that conversion is NADPH-dependent. NADPH comes from the pentose phosphate pathway, whose rate-limiting enzyme is G6PD. In a G6PD-deficient patient there is not enough NADPH to activate the drug, so the antidote that rescued Patient 1 does essentially nothing for Patient 2 — and the oxidant stress of the dye can worsen hemolysis.

Beyond this lecture: the existence of the NADPH/G6PD dependency, and the alternatives used when methylene blue is unsuitable, are clinical pharmacy content. They are included here because they show the lecture's Fe2+/Fe3+ chemistry deciding a real therapeutic choice — not because dosing or protocol detail is examinable from this module.

Module 5 · Case 3

Sickle cell anemia

A single amino acid substitution in the β chain produces hemoglobin S: glutamate at position 6 is replaced by valine (Glu6Val). A charged residue on the protein surface becomes a hydrophobic one.

  • Glu6Val → hydrophobic patch on the protein surface
  • That valine interacts with hydrophobic residues on a neighbouring hemoglobin molecule
  • Those partner residues are exposed only in the T state, so only deoxyhemoglobin aggregates
  • Fibrous aggregates → elongated, sickled, rigid red blood cells
  • Rigid cells clog small capillaries → impaired blood flow and tissue damage
  • Shortened red cell lifespan → anemia

Clinically, sickle cell anemia is associated with painful swelling of the extremities, higher risk of stroke, and bacterial infection.

Back to the framework

Everything that pushes hemoglobin toward the T state — low oxygen, low pH, high CO2 — is also a condition that makes HbS more likely to aggregate. The same T/R framework that explained oxygen delivery in Module 4 explains sickling risk here.

What the model is showing you

Fibers need both conditions at once. The β6 valine supplies a hydrophobic knob, but its partner pocket on the neighbouring β chain is only exposed in the T state. HbA never polymerizes because it has no knob; oxygenated HbS never polymerizes because there is no pocket to dock into. This is why sickling tracks every T-favoring condition from Module 4 — low oxygen, low pH, high CO2.

The HbF slider matters more than its size suggests because polymerization is steeply concentration-dependent: a modest fall in the HbS concentration produces a disproportionately large delay before fibers form. That relationship is the rationale behind therapies that raise HbF. Beyond this lecture: specific agents and dosing are clinical content, not examinable here.
